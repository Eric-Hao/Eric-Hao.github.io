InstantViR: distilling video restoration into a streaming solver

From iterative sampling to streaming reconstruction

A restoration system for live video cannot wait for a long denoising trajectory before displaying the next frame. Video diffusion models have strong learned knowledge of appearance and motion, but using that knowledge through repeated posterior sampling is expensive. Models trained for offline generation can also attend to future frames that a live stream has not delivered yet.

InstantViR changes both the computation and the temporal interface. A bidirectional video diffusion model acts as a teacher during training. A student learns to map degraded measurements directly to reconstructed video blocks in one forward pass, while attending only to the current block and previous blocks. The expensive optimization is amortized: its cost is paid during training and reused across subsequent inputs.

A second intervention replaces the video autoencoder. Once iterative diffusion is removed, decoding the student’s latent representation becomes a major part of the remaining runtime.

Combining measurement fidelity with a video prior

The method starts from a Bayesian inverse problem. The measurement model describes how a clean video would be blurred, masked, or downsampled. The video prior describes which candidate videos look plausible. Training a conditional student to approximate the posterior leads to an objective combining measurement fidelity with a regularizer derived from the frozen teacher.

For inpainting, imagine that half the observed pixels are missing. Reapplying the mask to the restored video should reproduce the visible pixels. That constraint alone says little about the missing half. The teacher supplies a learned preference for coherent video structure there. For deblurring, the corresponding check blurs the reconstruction and compares it with the observed blurred input.

Figure 2 shows this division directly: the data-likelihood branch evaluates consistency with the known operator, while the distillation branch evaluates the reconstruction through a video diffusion prior and an auxiliary score network. The paper approximates the prior objective using score-based training; it does not compute an exact posterior distribution. A single feed-forward result should therefore be understood as a learned approximation, not an exact solution to every inverse problem.

Causal blocks and their buffering cost

The student uses bidirectional attention inside the current temporal block and causal attention across blocks. It can combine all frames already present in the current block, then reuse keys and values from previously reconstructed blocks through a KV cache. It never needs to read an unseen future block.

Blockwise operation preserves local motion relationships and reduces repeated computation, but introduces a buffering interval. Frames per second measures throughput; it does not by itself specify first-frame latency, capture-to-display delay, or the effect of the chosen block size.

The teacher remains bidirectional during training. That asymmetry is intentional: the student receives training signals from a model with richer temporal context, while its deployed attention mask enforces the streaming constraint. The challenge is transferring the teacher’s useful video statistics without also transferring its dependence on future observations.

Adapting the teacher to a faster autoencoder

The original autoencoder leaves the distilled model at roughly fourteen frames per second in the reported benchmark. Replacing it with LeanVAE offers a faster encoder-decoder pair, but latent vectors from two different autoencoders need not mean the same thing. Applying the teacher’s score model directly to the new latent space would ask it to evaluate unfamiliar coordinates.

InstantViR addresses this mismatch during training by decoding the student’s new latent into pixels and re-encoding those pixels with the teacher’s original encoder. The teacher regularizer is then evaluated in its familiar latent space. At inference, the fast student and new decoder remain, without the teacher-side conversion path.

The teacher-side conversion is paid for during training and disappears at deployment. Changing representations still affects reconstruction quality, as the accelerated model’s results show.

Throughput and reconstruction quality

Table 1 reports 35.56 FPS for the LeanVAE variant, compared with 13.91 FPS for the original-VAE student and 0.29 FPS for SVI. The evaluation processes videos at 832 × 480. The abstract mentions A100 hardware, while Section 4.1 specifies a single A800 80 GB GPU for FPS measurement; that inconsistency should be retained when interpreting the speed claim.

The evaluation uses a held-out Open-Sora set and REDS30 for generalization. Table 1 reports lower Fréchet Video Distance for the accelerated model than SVI across inpainting, super-resolution, and deblurring. FVD measures video-distribution similarity and is influenced by temporal and visual properties; it is not a direct guarantee that every frame is faithful or flicker-free.

The spatial results are mixed in a way the headline obscures. In Table 2, four-times super-resolution PSNR is 34.91 dB with the original VAE but 27.04 dB with LeanVAE; VISION-XL reaches 35.69 dB. Conversely, accelerated inpainting reaches 31.78 dB compared with 30.54 dB for the original-VAE student. The fast variant cannot be described as uniformly matching its slower counterpart.

Training data, compute, and degradation assumptions

The training objective does not require clean target videos paired with each degraded measurement. Nevertheless, the reported experiment starts from 6,000 Open-Sora clips and generates measurements using known operators. “Paired-data-free” describes how supervision is used, not an absence of training video, a lack of degradation assumptions, or training without substantial compute. The paper reports two weeks on eight A100 GPUs.

Known degradation operators also distinguish these experiments from blind restoration of arbitrary internet video. A solver trained around masking or a specified blur model needs further validation when the actual corruption is unknown, nonlinear, or changes over time. Text guidance adds another distinction: it can produce plausible edits in missing regions, but those edits are not recovered evidence of what the original scene contained.

The choice between decoders therefore depends on the application. A live preview may favor throughput; an archival reconstruction may value the original decoder’s super-resolution fidelity enough to accept lower speed.
